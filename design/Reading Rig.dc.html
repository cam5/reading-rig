Reading Rig Capital, Volume I  ·  Ch. 1, The Commodity  ·  §4 37% · 4h left
Section four

The Fetishism of Commodities and the Secret Thereof

A commodity appears, at first sight, a very trivial thing, and easily understood. Its analysis shows that it is, in reality, a very queer thing, abounding in metaphysical subtleties and theological niceties.

So far as it is a value in use, there is nothing mysterious about it, whether we consider it from the point of view that by its properties it is capable of satisfying human wants, or from the point that those properties are the product of human labour.

It is as clear as noon-day, that man, by his industry, changes the forms of the materials furnished by Nature, in such a way as to make them useful to him. The form of wood, for instance, is altered, by making a table out of it. Yet, for all that, the table continues to be that common, every-day thing, wood.

But, so soon as it steps forth as a commodity, it is changed into something transcendent. It not only stands with its feet on the ground, but, in relation to all other commodities, it stands on its head, and evolves out of its wooden brain grotesque ideas, far more wonderful than table-turning ever was.

The table is still wood — the mystery isn't in the object. Keep this for the Adorno chapter.

If the table is still just wood, where exactly does the mystery enter?

Not in the wood, and not in the labour either — Marx has just conceded both are ordinary. It enters at the moment the table steps forth as a commodity: when a social relation between people takes the form of a relation between things.

0The brief, as I read it

Assumptions

Desktop web, 1440 × 900. Hi-fi static screens, not clickable yet.

Sample text is Capital, Vol. I, Ch. 1 §4 — Moore & Aveling, public domain. Secondary source shown as a user upload (Wood, Karl Marx).

Copy is quiet and literary: no exclamation, no product cheer, no emoji.

The system

Terracotta marks the machine's voice and the live thing; sage marks your hand and your shelf. Nothing else is coloured.

Every Rig answer can be pushed left into the gutter — that is the only way notes are made, so reading and thinking share one artefact.

Context is stated in plain sentences, never as a token count, and always includes what it is not looking at (past your bookmark).

Open questions

Does the reading pane ever go full-width — a “put the machine away” mode?

Should the commonplace book be a third view or a filter over the margin?

Serif for body text would suit a reader; Organic only sanctions Figtree, so I've stayed with it.
